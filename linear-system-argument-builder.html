Scenario

One question shell. Two providers, a fixed fee and a per-unit rate each. Two of these systems cross somewhere sensible, one never crosses at all, and one crosses at a number the situation cannot use.

The four moves

1 Set up — turn each sentence into c = rate × amount + fee. Both equations are built at once; an equation reads badly half-finished.

2 Compare — the two rates, then how often the costs can be equal. This is the linear analogue of the quadratic's discriminant step, and it is what lets the parallel case be answered without a "no solution" button inside the workspace.

3 Solve — a SystemOfEquationsTransformer-style workspace, then the two final-answer blanks that are actually graded. Only on the one-solution branch.

4 Interpret — keep or drop the solution, then say what it means.

Notes

Stage 1's four blanks are all visible at once — an equation reads badly half-built — while the sentence blanks later still arrive one at a time.

The keep-or-drop toggles appear on every branch that produced a solution, including the ordinary taxi case where the right move is to keep it. If they only showed up in the trick scenario they would be a tell. The line above them is neutral: nothing says why a negative answer might not belong.

A wrong claim at stage 2 still produces a complete argument that fails on Check, not a dead end. Claim "exactly one" on the parallel scenario and the workspace will happily reduce it to 5 = 9.

The graph appears only at the very end, and it never draws a marker for a solution the learner left off.

The solve workspace

Stage 3 is a small SystemOfEquationsTransformer: rows A and B, a bank of action pills, a step row per transformation, and Undo. Instead of dragging a pill onto a row, tap a row to make it active, then tap a pill.

The operand is typed on a small keyboard rather than chosen from a list, as it is in the real package — digits 1–5 then 6–0, a minus sign, the two variable letters, backspace and confirm. A fixed list of operands always leaves some equation with no sensible move; typing does not.

The intended route is Substitute A into B → Subtract d → Subtract 3 → Substitute B into A → Combine. Add and Subtract take a constant or a variable term, so −d is a legal move.

It runs real algebra, not a script — any legal move is allowed, and illegal ones are refused with a reason ("That would leave fractions", "Substitute needs the other equation to have a single variable on its own").

The workspace is scaffolding; the final-answer blanks are the commitment. Nothing is auto-filled from the working-out.
