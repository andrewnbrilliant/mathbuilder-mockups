Why evidence, not just a label

Naming a sequence increasing is a guess until it is tied to a claim about every consecutive pair.

“Neither” is the interesting answer, because it is the one that has to be shown. The learner assembles their own comparison out of parts, and anything that rules out both increasing and decreasing is accepted.

Notes

The depth of the argument is chosen by the learner. Two blanks settle increasing or decreasing. “Neither” needs a comparison built from parts, and a second one only if the first was an inequality — an equality has already done the whole job.

Evidence is graded by what it rules out, never against an answer key. A true rise rules out decreasing, a true fall rules out increasing, an equality rules out both; the argument passes when both are ruled out. So a2 > a1 with a5 < a3 passes, even though the indices neither touch nor line up.

Nothing is listed for the learner to read off. No table of terms and no ready-made comparisons — choosing which two terms to compare is most of the thinking, so it cannot be handed over in the bank.

A wrong first choice still finishes. Call a decreasing sequence increasing and you are handed the same evidence sentence, build a complete argument, and it fails on Check — with the red box on the claim that actually broke.
